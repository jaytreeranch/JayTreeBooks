Chapter One

The trick to fixing an obituary is that you don't fix the dead. You fix the living, who have to keep reading it.

I want that understood up front, because people who don't do this work assume the opposite — that the dead are the delicate thing, that you handle their names the way you'd handle a bird's egg, and the living can take whatever's written about them because they're still around to argue. It's the other way. The dead don't care if you spell it Kathleen or Katheryn. The dead have stopped keeping score. It's the daughter who has to open the bound volume forty years from now, looking for her mother, and finds a stranger's name looking back at her, who cares. So you fix it for her. You fix it so carefully that she never has to know it was ever wrong, which is its own kind of mercy, and also, though I would not have put it this way in March, its own kind of lie.

I was three hours into a Tuesday and one correction into a Wednesday deadline when I found the mistake in the Cormier obituary, February 1962, page four of the Sentinel, bound in green buckram gone the color of pond water. Grace Cormier, survived by her husband Neil and her daughter Katheryn, the volume said. The county death index, which I'd pulled for an unrelated reason and left open on the desk out of habit, said Kathleen.

This is the part of the job nobody finds interesting when I describe it, and it's the part I like best. Not the mystery of it — there's no mystery in a 1962 typesetter's fat thumb hitting the y key eight rows down from the a, or in seventy years of nobody noticing because nobody but a nine-year-old girl who grew into an eighty-year-old woman was ever going to look that closely at her own mother's name. There's no mystery. There's just the fact of it, sitting there wrong for six decades, waiting for someone with the patience to be bothered.

I got out the ledger — the real one, the physical one, the one that exists because a hundred years ago somebody decided a small Maine paper mill town needed its own permanent record and then never got around to deciding it didn't, not even after the internet made the whole apparatus technically unnecessary — and I logged it the way Marion taught me. Date of discrepancy. Date of source document. Nature of error. Correction applied. Corrector initials.

A.V., I wrote, my own hand smaller and tighter than my mother's had been, and I want to be honest that there is a pleasure in that, in the small violence of getting it right after everyone else stopped trying. I've never known what to call it besides pride, though pride isn't really the word, because pride implies you did something, and all I'd done was notice.

I took the fine brush and the bottle of correction fluid — not the cheap stuff, the archival kind, buffered to match the paper's pH so it won't yellow different from the page around it in another sixty years, ordered special from a conservator's supply house in Vermont because apparently there is a company that still makes this, still ships it to the four or five towns in America stubborn enough to still need it — and I painted out the y. Small stroke, steady hand. Waited the ninety seconds it needs. Took up the pen, archival ink, and wrote the l where the y had been, in a hand as close to the 1962 typesetter's as I could manage after eight years of practice, which by now is close enough that if you didn't know to look, you would never know to look.

Kathleen Cormier. Fixed.

I sat there a second with the pen still in my hand, the way you do after you've finished something, waiting to see if the feeling that comes after it is satisfaction or just the absence of the thing that was bothering you. Mostly it's the second one. I've stopped expecting it to be the first.

That's the job. That's been the job since 2018, when my mother's heart quit on her on a Sunday in October and I found out, going through her desk at the library three weeks later, looking for insurance paperwork, that she had a second desk. A locked one. That there was a whole practice, unwritten anywhere, unpaid by anyone, that the town of Averill Falls had apparently agreed a long time ago somebody ought to be doing, and that somebody had for thirty years been my mother, and now, God help me, was going to be me.

Nobody hired me for it. There's no job posting for Corrector, no interview, no committee. You inherit it the way you inherit a family plot at the cemetery — quietly, with paperwork nobody explains, and by the time you understand what you've taken on, you're already standing in it.

The archive is in the basement of the Averill Falls Public Library, which is a Carnegie building, red brick, built in 1911 with money from a man who never once set foot in this town and whose name is on a bronze plaque nobody reads. Upstairs it's a library like any library — story hour on Tuesdays, a returns bin that jams if you don't lift the lid at the right angle, Warren Pryce behind the desk with his glasses pushed up into his hair because he's always just been doing something that required them off. Downstairs it's something else. Downstairs is where the town keeps what it's decided is true.

Bound volumes of the Sentinel back to 1911, the year the building went up, water-stained along the bottom edge from a flood I wasn't alive for. Microfiche cabinets nobody's opened in a decade because everything's digitized now, technically, except the town has never trusted digital the way it trusts paper, so the originals stay, filed and re-filed, dusted twice a year. Land records, duplicate copies of things the county already has, kept here too, because Averill Falls has never in its history been comfortable letting anyone else be the only place holding the truth about it. Class photographs in a corridor that floods first, every flood, going back to the twenties, boys in collars too big for them, girls with their hair pinned the way somebody's mother thought was nice.

It smells like a specific thing down there that I've never smelled anywhere else — old paper and river damp and, underneath it, faint, the mineral smell of the concrete itself, which sits eleven feet below the waterline of a river that has never once, in a hundred and fifty years, agreed to stay where it's told.

My office is off the back of it, a door that used to say STORAGE in stenciled paint before my mother had it repainted, simply, VOSS. I haven't changed it. I tell myself that's because there's no reason to spend money on a new sign. I know that isn't why.

Her desk is still her desk. I use it — I'm not sentimental in the way that would keep a desk empty for eight years out of grief, or at least I don't think I am — but I've never fully cleared it out, either. There's a drawer I don't open. I'll get to that. Everyone who tells a story like mine says that at some point, there's a drawer I don't open, and I used to think that was a kind of narrative laziness, real life being tidier than that, and I want to be honest that it is not narrative laziness. Some drawers really do sit there for eight years daring you.

I finished the Cormier correction a little after four, cleaned the brush, capped the fluid, and was writing the day's log entry — routine, minor, self-discovered — when I heard the front door upstairs open and shut, and then Warren's voice, muffled through the floor, saying something to somebody, and then a silence that went on a beat too long before I heard him coming down the stairs faster than Warren usually moves.

He didn't come into the office. He stopped in the archive doorway, one hand still on the frame, and he had an envelope in the other hand that he was not quite holding out to me and not quite holding away from me either.

"This was under the front door," he said. "Not the mail slot. Under the door."

I want to say I felt something then, some prickle up the back of the neck that a better-written version of me would have felt, the kind of thing you're supposed to feel when the story is about to start. I didn't. I felt annoyed, mostly, the way you do when someone hands you one more thing at four in the afternoon on a day you'd already decided was finished.

"Okay," I said.

"Del." He still didn't come in. "It's got your initials on it. The way they do it."

That got my attention, if not for the reason it should have. There is exactly one kind of correspondence in Averill Falls addressed to A.V. in that particular blunt, level, unmistakably manual-typewriter font, struck slightly unevenly the way a real machine strikes, not a printer doing its best impression of one — and it's the kind that comes maybe six, eight times a year, unsigned, unstamped, left where I'll find it rather than mailed where a postmark could date it.

Correction slips.

I'd gotten one in January. A wrong hyphenation in a 1970s zoning notice, forty minutes of work, forgotten by February. I'd gotten two the year before. This was early for a second one, and I remember thinking that, specifically — it's early — the way you'd think it about a bill arriving before you expected it, some small bookkeeping unease and nothing more.

Warren finally crossed the room and set it on the desk in front of me instead of handing it to me directly, which I noticed and filed away without deciding yet what it meant. He does that. He'll drive a thing halfway to you and then let you finish the distance yourself, and I used to think it was a librarian's fastidiousness about other people's belongings and I have since learned it is not that at all.

"You want me to stay?" he said.

"For a correction slip?"

"For whatever it is."

"It's a correction slip, Warren."

He looked at it a second longer than the sentence needed. Then he nodded, the way he does when he's decided not to say the next thing, and went back upstairs, and I heard the returns bin jam and un-jam itself under his hand two floors up, and I sat there with the envelope in the yellow lamplight of my mother's desk in my mother's basement in a town that keeps its own second set of every record just in case, and I opened it.

One sheet. Same stock as always, the cheap cotton bond nobody's manufactured new in thirty years, which meant whoever typed this was either old enough to have a supply of it or careful enough to have found one. Same machine — I'd know that crooked lowercase e anywhere by now, the way it sat half a hair below the baseline every single time, a defect specific to one specific typewriter somewhere in this town that I had never once, in eight years, managed to locate.

It read:

CORRECTION REQUIRED. AVERILL FALLS SENTINEL, JUNE 14, 2003. "LOCAL MAN DROWNS IN BOATING MISHAP." NAME OF DECEASED INCORRECTLY RECORDED AS DANIEL J. FERRIS. SUBJECT DID NOT DROWN. RECORD TO BE AMENDED ACCORDINGLY.

I read it twice. The first time for the words. The second time for the thing under the words, which I couldn't have named yet, sitting there at four-fifteen with the lamp on and the river a hundred yards away doing what it always does this time of year, restless under the last of the ice, and which I would spend a great deal of the next several months learning to name.

It didn't say what had happened to Danny Ferris instead of drowning. It only said the record was wrong.

I remember I checked the date twice — June 14, 2003 — because Danny Ferris was not a stranger to me the way most of the names in the ledger are. I'd gone to school two grades behind his sister. I remembered, or thought I remembered, standing at the edge of a crowd outside the Falls Grill the week it happened, listening to the adults use the particular flattened voice they use for a drowning, because a drowning on the Kenderee is not news in this town so much as weather, an event the river produces on its own schedule the way it produces flood and thaw, something you grieve and then absorb rather than something you investigate.

I pulled the bound volume for June 2003 off the shelf without deciding to. It's a habit older than the doubt that should have gone with it — you don't take a correction slip's word for anything, you verify, that's the entire discipline of the job, Marion drilled that into me before I understood why it mattered as much as it did. Page three. LOCAL MAN DROWNS IN BOATING MISHAP. A photograph, grainy, of the boat launch below the mill. Daniel J. Ferris, 34, of Averill Falls, survived by his wife Patrice and by his mother, Louise.

I looked a long time at that photograph, at the black water and the gray sky and the small aluminum boat pulled up crooked on the bank, and at nothing in it that told me anything except that a man had drowned twenty-three years ago in a town that has never had any trouble at all believing its river takes what it takes.

I did not, that afternoon, make the correction.

I put the slip in the drawer where I keep the current ones, the ones waiting on verification, and I told myself I'd get to it in the morning, when there was more daylight in the basement and less of whatever this feeling was, sitting low in my chest like something swallowed the wrong way.

Upstairs, the returns bin jammed a third time. I heard Warren swear at it, quietly, the way he does, and I sat a while longer at my mother's desk with the lamp burning and the ledger open to a page from 1962 where a dead woman's name was, for the first time in sixty-four years, spelled the way her daughter had always known it should be, and I thought: that one, at least, I got right.

I had no way of knowing yet how much that would come to matter to me later — that there was a version of this job where getting it right and doing the right thing were the same motion, and a version where they were not, and that I had, for eight quiet years, been allowed to believe I only ever did the first kind.
