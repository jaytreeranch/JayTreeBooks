The Hollow Bell

A Millbrook Falls Mystery

 

Prologue

 

The water has always been patient with her, which is more than she can say for herself.

Forty-three years she has stood at the edge of it, in every season, at every hour, and she has never once heard it demand anything of her that she did not already, in some buried chamber of herself, believe it was owed. That is the thing nobody in this town has ever understood, watching her carry casseroles to the grieving, watching her catalog its slow economic death into careful acid-free folders, watching her be, for four decades, the softest and steadiest hand this particular corner of Maine has ever known. They think patience is a virtue she practices. They do not understand that patience is the only shape her grief has ever been permitted to take.

She will tell you, if you ask her plainly — and almost no one, in forty-three years, has ever asked her plainly — that she does not hate this town. She loves it with a ferocity that has cost her a marriage, a comfortable retirement, every ordinary joy a woman her age might otherwise expect to hold. She loves it the way a person loves a wound they have tended so long they can no longer imagine themselves without it. And she has kept, faithfully, quietly, at a cost she alone has ever had to reckon, the one account this town has never had the courage to keep honestly for itself: the plain, unbearable fact that some debts do not care whether anyone remembers agreeing to them.

A body is coming up out of the ice this winter. She has known it would, eventually — ice always gives back what it takes, in its own patient time, on a schedule no living person gets to set. She does not yet know that this particular giving-back will be the beginning of the end of everything she has spent forty-three years building. She knows only that the water is finally, after twenty years, ready to talk, and that she, as she has done every single time before, will need to be the one standing closest when it does.

PART ONE: THE ICE
CHAPTER ONE: WHAT THE ICE GAVE UP

The first hard freeze of the year always changed the sound of Millbrook Falls, and Warden Pete Aldous had lived beside that sound for thirty-one years. In summer the falls roared. In autumn they muttered. By late December, when the cold finally closed its fist around the gorge, the water went quiet under two feet of ice, and the silence was the thing that woke you up at night, not the noise.

So when Pete heard the crack — a long, splitting groan like a door opening in an old house — he was already awake, already reaching for his boots.

It was 5:40 in the morning on the second of December, dark as the frozen gorge below, the kind of darkness that erased distance, eleven degrees on the thermometer nailed to his porch post. He told himself it was just the ice settling. Ice did that. He’d heard it a thousand times.

He went out anyway.

The beam of his flashlight found the ice shelf below the falls first, humped and blistered the way it always was before the real cold smoothed it flat.

Then it found the dark shape frozen into the surface, ten feet out from the bank, and Pete Aldous stood very still, doing the thing the mind does when it is shown something it does not want to be true: he tried to make it a log. A trash bag. A dead deer.

It was none of those things. It was a hand, palm up, fingers slightly curled, suspended eight inches under clear black ice, and above the hand, deeper in the murk where his light could not quite reach, the pale smear of a face turned toward the surface as if it had been looking up at the sky the whole time it went under.

Pete Aldous did not go closer. He had grown up in Millbrook Falls, had heard every story his grandmother told about the bell in the old Congregational church — and some deep, unreasoning part of him understood, before the state police ever arrived, before anyone said a word about dental records, that the ice had not given up a stranger.

He was right. It would take four days to be sure, and when they were sure, the certainty would open a door in the town that had been swollen shut for twenty years.

Sheriff Del Hutchins got the call at 6:12 a.m. and was standing at the lip of the gorge by 6:40, watching the state crime scene unit work a chainsaw through eighteen inches of ice with the grim, unhurried care that told him this was not a rescue.

Del was fifty-four, sheriff of this county for eleven years, long enough to know that Millbrook Falls did not, as a rule, produce bodies. It produced potholes and poached deer and the occasional bar fight outside the Moose Lodge. The falls had claimed exactly one person in his tenure, a drunk college kid in 2019 who’d tried to cross the ice on a dare, and the memory of pulling that boy out, blue and stiff as a fence post, had never fully left him.

This was worse. He could tell that much before they’d even freed the body, just from the way Dr. Okafor, the medical examiner up from Augusta, kept crouching at the edge of the cut ice and going still.

It took the crime scene unit the better part of three hours to free her — a careful, almost surgical process once the initial shock of discovery gave way to procedure, technicians cutting a wide perimeter around the body rather than through it, working in from the edges so the ice itself could be lifted nearly whole and photographed before anyone touched what lay beneath. Del stood at the lip of the gorge the entire time, hands jammed in his coat pockets against a cold that had stopped registering as cold an hour in, and found himself doing the arithmetic he would do a dozen more times before the day was through: twenty years, this coming October. A girl who would have been thirty-six now, who had instead been sixteen for two full decades, preserved in black water while the rest of the town went on aging around her.

“How old,” Del said. Not a question, exactly. More of a brace.

“Based on preservation and the clothing — and I want a full lab workup before anyone quotes me — I’d guess this individual has been in that ice a long time. Years, Sheriff. Not months.” She straightened up, wincing at her own knees. “I’ll know more in Augusta. But Del — you’re going to want your missing persons file. All of it, not just the recent stuff.”

Del Hutchins looked out at the black gash in the ice and thought of exactly one name.

Marnie Doucette. Sixteen years old. Vanished from the footbridge above these falls on the night of the Harvest Fair, October the eleventh, twenty years ago come next fall. He’d been a deputy then, twenty-three and green as a spring onion, and he had walked this gorge with a search line of forty volunteers for six straight days and found nothing. The case had gone cold the way cases in small towns go cold — not from lack of caring, but from lack of anywhere left to look.

“Del.” Okafor’s voice again, gentler now. “I didn’t say who it was. Don’t get ahead of the evidence.”

“I know,” Del said, and did not believe himself even a little.

Three hundred miles south, in an apartment above a laundromat in Boston, Claire Renner was staring at a blinking cursor on a half-finished magazine piece when her phone rang with a number she hadn’t seen in eleven years.

Millbrook Falls Sheriff’s Department.

She let it ring twice before she picked it up. “This is Claire.”

“Claire. It’s Del Hutchins.” A pause, long enough that she could hear wind on his end, some outdoor sound. “I need you to sit down, if you’re not already.”

She was standing. She sat down anyway, right there on the cold kitchen tile. “You found her,” Claire said.

“We found somebody,” Del said. “In the gorge below the falls. I’m not going to tell you it’s her, because I don’t know that yet. But I think you should come home, Claire.”

Outside her window, a bus hissed to a stop. Claire closed her eyes and saw the footbridge on an October night twenty years gone, and her sister’s laugh carrying back to her over the sound of the water — the last sound of her sister Claire had ever actually heard, rather than remembered. “I’m coming,” she said.
