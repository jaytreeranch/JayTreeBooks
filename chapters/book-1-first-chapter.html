Second Draft

PART ONE: NORA

Chapter One

 

People think confession is supposed to be honest. In my experience, it's mostly rehearsed.

I've run a business out of my sunporch for six years now called Second Draft. The pitch, if you can call it that — I've never once advertised, everyone who finds me is sent by somebody who found me first — is simple. Nobody says the true, hard thing right on the first try. Not the good ones and not the terrible ones. You think a man gets "I forgive you" right on the first attempt, forty years overdue, standing over his mother's casket? He needs a run at it first. A place to be clumsy where it doesn't cost him anything. That's what I sell: a room to be clumsy in.

They sit across from me in the wicker chair that's outlasted two coats of porch paint and one marriage, and they tell somebody who isn't anybody the thing they can't yet tell the person who is. I ask the ordinary questions — where were you standing, what could you smell, what was she wearing, what time did the light come through the window — because detail is where the truth keeps its splinters. A lie stays smooth if you don't handle it too much. A true thing snags.

I used to tell new clients, half joking — the way you joke about things you'd rather not think about too hard — that if you got the details exact enough, precise enough, true enough, you stopped rehearsing the moment and started building it. I don't say that anymore. Not since Roy.

I don't ask for real names. That's rule one, and I tell every client on the phone before I ever see their face: use whatever name feels true, not whichever one's on your driver's license. It isn't only a legal thing. It's that people are braver behind a small mask than they are behind their own name, and I am in the business of borrowed courage.

In six years I have coached a man through telling his mother he's an atheist, a woman through firing the business partner who was also her college roommate, a teenage boy through coming out to a father who kept a shotgun mounted over the fireplace like a piece of framed art. I have played wives, husbands, dead fathers, living ones, a dog once, at a client's insistence, because he said the dog was the only one who'd actually understand him. Mostly, when they leave my porch, they aren't fixed. Rehearsal doesn't fix anything. It just means they don't flinch as hard when the curtain goes up for real.

Roy came to me on the first Wednesday of September, sent, he said, by a friend from a group he didn't name, for a reason he didn't name either. That's not unusual. What was unusual, a little, was that he sat down before I'd finished offering him coffee and said: "I need to tell somebody I did something. And I need to know if she can still love me after."

I asked who she was. He said it didn't matter. He said I should just be her.

That's fine. That's more than fine — that's most Wednesdays. I asked him where I was standing, as her. He said the kitchen. I asked what time of day. He said early, before the sun was all the way up, when everything's still that flat grey color. I asked what I could smell. He thought about it a long time and said: dirt. Cut tomato vines. Somebody two doors down burning leaves too early in the season.

He didn't get much further than that, the first time. He cried without any tears coming, which I've seen before — grief that's dried out from being carried too long — and I wrote him up afterward the way I write everyone up, in a composition notebook I keep in the kitchen drawer: Roy. 9/3. Confession, unspecified. Affect flat, controlled. Revisit sensory detail next time.

Outside, on the porch beam, the sea-glass chime my ex-husband bolted up the summer before he left us both, me and the porch, clinked against itself in the wind, the way it does in any wind at all. I didn't think about it again until much later.

---

He came back on the seventeenth, same time, same chair. I remember because I'd started a fresh page and dated it before he'd even sat down, which wasn't like me — usually I wait until after, let the session tell me what's worth writing.

"Same girl?" I asked. Casual. You keep it light at the start, let them ease into the water instead of diving.

"Same everything," he said, and sat, and I noticed for the first time that when he was working himself up to something, he pressed his thumbnail into the pad of his own index finger, hard enough to leave a little white crescent that went pink, then red, while I watched. He did it three times that session. I wrote it down as a nervous habit. Nervous habits are useful — they tell you where the pressure's building.

We picked up where we'd left off: the kitchen, the window, the mailbox he still hadn't described in any color. I asked him to walk through what happens right after he says it. What she does. What he does. He got as far as "she goes very quiet, and I can hear the—" and stopped, thumbnail digging in, and I said, gently, take your time, and the room did something I didn't have a word for yet.

It wasn't the light, exactly, though the light was part of it — that flat grey going flatter, greyer, like a cloud sliding over a sun that had already been mostly clouded. It was more that the ten seconds it took him to finish that sentence lasted longer than ten seconds ought to, the way a dream stretches without your noticing until you try to describe it after and the math doesn't work. When he finally said "— I can hear the chime," I heard the chime. Not remembered hearing it. Not anticipated it. Heard it, right then, one clean note, through a closed window, on a still afternoon, with the maples in the yard not moving at all.

I told myself the same thing I'd tell any client who spooked themselves with their own good work: the mind fills in sound the way it fills in a word you're sure was on the tip of your tongue. That's what good rehearsal does. It gets so real your body starts responding to it like it's real. That's the whole point of the exercise.

I believed that, that day. I want that on the record.

---

He came back after that too, most Wednesdays through the end of September and on into October, and by the middle of the month I had a good stack of pages on him in the composition notebook, and the story he was rehearsing had gone from a shape to a place. The kitchen had a window over the sink. The window looked out on a mailbox painted the good hunter green, not the ugly green they sell at the hardware store now. There was a dog three houses down that barked at the mail truck and nothing else, ever, like it had made its peace with every other danger in the world. There were tomato plants, staked with old broom handles because real stakes cost money she didn't want to spend. There was a wind chime.

I want to be honest about something: none of that struck me as strange, not really, not the way it should read to you now. I live in a town with maybe six thousand people in it. There are a lot of green mailboxes. There are a lot of dogs that only bark at the mail truck. I have tomato plants staked with old broom handles because I, too, am cheap in exactly that specific way, and I told myself that was simply what frugal gardening looked like in a town like this one — that I wasn't special, that any hundred yards within a mile of my porch probably had the same three details sitting in it. I want you to understand I am not a stupid woman. I want you to understand that dismissing it was the correct thing to do, statistically, right up until it wasn't.

By the third week of October, I'd started doing something I've never done with a client before or since: I started testing him.

I don't know what I thought I was testing, not exactly, not in words I would have said out loud to another person. I told myself I was checking his consistency, the way you'd check any story for holes, because that's good practice, a good coach probes for the seams. That Wednesday, when he got to the part about the window over the sink, I said: "The mailbox. Walk me through it again. I have it as green in my notes."

"It is green," he said. "Hunter green. The good kind."

"Try blue," I said. "For me. Just to see how it sits."

He looked at me the way you'd look at someone who'd asked you to try describing your own mother with somebody else's face. Not offended. Confused, the way you're confused when the ground doesn't behave the way ground is supposed to.

"It's not blue," he said. "It's green. It's always been green."

I pushed harder than I should have. I told him it was an exercise, that changing a fixed detail sometimes shakes something loose underneath it, and reluctantly, like a man reciting a phone number he doesn't believe in, he said: "The mailbox is blue," and the word came out of him wrong, flat, a stone dropped down a dry well.

We moved on. I told myself I'd made my point, whatever my point was, and when he left I told myself I wouldn't even look, because looking would mean I'd taken it seriously, and I hadn't, not really, not in the daylight.

I looked anyway. I always look, every evening, it's four steps off the porch and down the walk, close enough that I don't think about doing it any more than I think about breathing — except that evening I did think about it, walking down my own front walk in the early dark with the first real cold snap of the year sitting in my lungs, and my own mailbox, hunter green for eleven years, the same green as the porch trim, stopped me dead on the walk before I was halfway to it.

It wasn't blue. I want to be careful here, because I've turned this over enough times to distrust my own memory of it, which tells you something about what this whole business does to a person's confidence in what she saw. It wasn't blue. But it wasn't right, either. There was a paleness to it under the streetlight, a kind of primer-grey undercoat showing through in patches, the way a paint job looks in the hour before the final coat goes on. Like the green hadn't finished deciding to be green yet.

I stood there long enough that my neighbor's porch light came on, and by the time I made myself walk close enough to touch it, run my hand down the cold metal side of it, it was green. Fully, uniformly, boringly green, the paint smooth and unbroken under my palm, not a fleck of primer anywhere.

I did not sleep well that night. I want that on the record too.

---

The first time he almost gave her a name was the last Wednesday in October, while he was describing the moment just before he says the terrible thing to her. Where's her attention, I asked him, standard question, keep her hands busy so the words can come out sideways instead of head-on. He said she'd be watering something. Tomatoes, probably, if it was that time of morning. She waters before work every day, rain or not, because her mother always said a plant knows if you skip a day, holds a grudge like a—

"N—" he said, and stopped, and this time when his thumbnail found the pad of his finger it went deep enough to well up a single dark bead of blood, and he didn't seem to feel it, and I didn't say anything, because you don't, not when somebody's that close to the truth.

"Noreen," he said. "Sorry. I had a — that's a different woman, different conversation, wrong file. Long week." He laughed, a small apologetic sound, and I laughed too, because that's the job, and that night I wrote in the notebook: near-slip, "N" name, covered smoothly. Possible tell, or possible nothing.

I underlined possible nothing twice. I told myself the mailbox had been streetlight and a long day and tired eyes doing what tired eyes do. I wanted both of those things to be true so badly that I made them true for another two weeks.

---

By the second Wednesday of November I'd decided, quietly, that I was going to stop seeing him. I didn't have a clean reason, nothing I could have put on an invoice. He'd never touched me, never raised his voice, never once been anything other than the saddest, gentlest man to ever sit in my wicker chair. But I had started, without deciding to, checking that the porch door was locked before his Wednesdays. I had started angling the composition notebook so he couldn't read it upside down, which he had never once tried to do.

I told him, that Wednesday, as kindly as I could manage, that I thought he'd gotten as far as he could get with me — that maybe a different kind of professional, a real one, though I didn't say that part, could take him the rest of the way. Before he could leave, I tried, one last time, to get something real out of him.

"Who exactly sent you to me?" I asked. I don't usually ask. Rule one cuts both ways; I don't just withhold my clients' names from the world, I try not to go digging for the world's names either. But I wanted, badly, one thread I could pull that led somewhere.

"A group," he said, the way he'd said it the first day.

"What kind of group?"

He thought about that longer than the question should have needed. "People who didn't get to finish something," he said finally. "Before it was too late to say it right."

"Where do you meet?"

"We don't, really. Not anymore. Not most of us." He said it the way you'd state a fact about the weather, and something in his face closed like a door easing shut under its own weight, and I understood I wasn't getting anything else out of that vein, and I let it go, because some part of me — the part that had already decided, without telling the rest of me, that it did not want confirmation of what it was starting to suspect — was relieved to let it go.

He took the rest of it better than I expected. He nodded, said he understood, said I'd helped him more than he probably knew, and stood up to leave, and I felt something in my chest unclench for the first time in a month.

At the door, with his hand already on the frame, he turned back.

"You should get that third step fixed," he said. "The one from the bottom. It's going to give out on somebody."

I have eleven wooden steps leading up to that porch. The third one from the bottom has creaked under every foot that's touched it for the four years since frost heaved the footing out of true, and I have never once mentioned it to a living soul, because it's a small ugly fact about a house I can't afford to fix properly, and you don't hand strangers the small ugly facts about your house. You especially don't hand them to a man who has paid you in cash every Wednesday for two months and never once given you a way to find him again.

I said thank you. I don't know why I said thank you. He left, and I stood in my kitchen a long time without turning on any lights, listening to the chime knock against itself in a wind I hadn't noticed picking up, and I told myself several things, one after another, the way you do. That old porches creak, and any observant person notices. That Noreen is a perfectly ordinary name a person can simply know, for perfectly ordinary reasons. That paint plays tricks under sodium light and tired eyes see primer that was never there. That I was a professional who had sat across from four hundred strangers doing exactly this, and this was, statistically, always going to be the one that unsettled me, the way a long enough run of green lights eventually ends at a red one.

I didn't call anyone. I want to be honest about that too. I didn't have a name to give them that wasn't a placeholder, or an address, or a license plate, or one single fact that wouldn't have sounded, out loud, in a police station, like a woman describing a bad feeling and nothing more.

He didn't call for seventeen days. I know because I counted, the way you count days since a diagnosis, without meaning to — your body just keeps the number for you whether you want it kept or not. By day fourteen I had nearly stopped flinching at the phone. By day seventeen I had almost convinced myself he'd taken my advice and found somebody else's porch to sit on, and underneath the relief I felt something smaller and uglier that I'm not proud of: a kind of professional disappointment. An unfinished case.

He called on the eighteenth day and asked, in the same gentle voice as always, whether he could come one more time. He said he thought he was finally ready to run the whole thing straight through, start to finish, the way you do a final dress rehearsal the night before the curtain goes up for real. He said he owed me that much, after everything — a clean ending, a finished draft, instead of leaving me with a stack of somebody else's unfinished business cluttering up my notebook.

I told him yes.

I have turned that Wednesday over more times than I can count, trying to build the version where I say no, and I can't build it. Some of it was fear, the plain animal kind that insists the safest place for a dangerous thing is somewhere you can see it, on your own schedule, in your own house, rather than loose somewhere you can't. Some of it, God help me, was still the job — the same reflex that makes you finish a stitch once you've started one, because a half-closed cut is worse than an open one. And some of it, the ugliest part, was that I still needed to know. I had a woman's whole ordinary life sitting in my kitchen drawer, a green mailbox and a lazy dog and a bad porch step, and I could not stand not knowing, one way or the other, whether I'd spent three months being handed a stranger's grief, or my own.

---

He came the first Wednesday of December. The tomato plants were long since pulled and composted; the broom-handle stakes were still out there, though, three grey bones leaning against each other in the frozen dirt, because some years you just don't get around to it. He looked at them through the window before he sat down. I saw him look.

He didn't need my prompting this time. That was the first wrongness — in three months I had never once let him run more than a page without stopping him for detail, for texture, for the splinter under the smooth lie, and this time he simply began, low and even, and didn't stop, and I understood somewhere in the second minute that I wasn't going to be allowed to interrupt. That this was the performance, finally, and I had been demoted all the way down to audience.

I noticed, too, that his hands were still. In three months I had never once seen him go this deep without his thumbnail finding the pad of his finger, without that small private wound working itself open and closed. Now his hands lay flat on his knees the way a man's hands lie when he's already said the hard thing and is only waiting, politely, for the room to catch up. I understood it before I let myself understand it: you don't rehearse with your whole nervous system anymore once the thing you're rehearsing has stopped being a maybe.

He told it in the kitchen. The good green mailbox. The dog three doors down that only ever barked at the mail truck. The wind chime, sea glass on brass hooks, a gift from somebody who hadn't stayed around to see if it lasted. He told the eleven wooden steps, and the third one from the bottom that's been giving a little for four years, that nobody's ever fixed, because you don't spend money on the thing that only scares you a little.

Somewhere in the middle of it the temperature on my own sunporch dropped enough that I watched my breath hang for a second before it thinned away, though the thermostat, when I checked it hours later, read sixty-eight the whole time, same as it always reads. The heavy curtain over the side window — I don't own the kind that moves, they hang dead straight even with the storm door standing open — lifted once, like something had leaned past it on its way somewhere else, and settled.

He told the part where she's standing at the sink with her hands wet, watering something that's already dead for the season, doing it anyway because her mother always said a plant holds a grudge if you skip a day, and how the light comes through flat and grey, and how he finally says the thing he's been building toward for three months of Wednesdays — and this time, for the first time since that first Wednesday in September, he didn't say her.

He said Nora.

He looked up when he said it. Not through me, the way he had for three months. At me. Like an actor finding his mark. Like he was checking I was still standing in position to receive the line.

I understood, sitting there, why I don't say that thing anymore, the thing I used to tell new clients back when this was still only a business and not also, apparently, whatever else it turned out to be. That if you get the details exact enough, precise enough, true enough, you stop rehearsing the moment and start building it. I used to think I meant it as encouragement. I have since revised my opinion of my own methods considerably, and it is far too late to be revising anything at all.

The chime knocked against itself on the porch beam, the ordinary small sound it makes in any wind at all, and neither of us moved, and after a while — I couldn't tell you how long; time does something strange to itself in a moment like that, stretches out thin as the skin on hot milk — he stood, the way he always stood, and thanked me, the way he always thanked me, for giving him somewhere safe to get it wrong until he got it right.

"Same time next week?" he said, at the door, the way he always said it.

It wasn't a question. I understood that the way you understand a held breath is about to become a scream — in your body a full second before your mind catches up. It hadn't been a question in weeks. It had only ever sounded like one because I needed it to.

---

I spent that week trying to build the version of the story where I don't open the door.

I called a woman I know two towns over and told her I might come stay a few days, didn't say why, and she said of course, her guest room was made up, come whenever — and then Thursday my car wouldn't turn over, a dead battery, the kind of ordinary failure that happens to ordinary cars, and by the time the man from the garage had it running again it was Friday, and I told myself Monday, I'll go Monday, and Monday I woke with a fever of a hundred and one that broke by noon and left me hollowed out and unwilling to trust myself on the highway, and by Tuesday I had stopped making the calls I told myself I was still going to make.

I want to tell you something got in the way. That some hand large enough to move batteries and viruses around a chessboard was keeping me on that porch on purpose. I've turned that over too, more than anything else in this whole account, more even than the mailbox. It would almost be a comfort, believing that. It would mean none of it was ever really a choice.

The truer thing, the thing I make myself write down because rule one of this business was always tell the truth once you've finally started telling it, is that I don't know. Cars die. Fevers break. Maybe I only needed to believe I couldn't leave badly enough that I made it true, the same way Roy, all those Wednesdays, made a mailbox true by wanting it badly enough, with enough specific, aching detail, to be real.

Maybe that's what this whole business was for, all along. Not rehearsal. Practice believing the thing you're most afraid of, until belief does the rest of the work for you.

He came on the second Wednesday of December, same time, and this time he didn't sit down.

He stood in the doorway to the sunporch with the cold coming in around him off the yard, and he looked, for the first time in three months, entirely calm — not sad, not gentle, just settled, the way a stage looks once the set's finished and there's nothing left to build. I remember thinking, absurdly, that he'd finally stopped being an actor and become, simply, a man doing the next thing on a list.

"I don't need the chair today," he said, and crossed the porch faster than a man his size should have been able to cross anything, and the composition notebook was already falling, already forgotten, by the time his hand closed around the back of my neck the way you'd grip something you'd practiced gripping a hundred times in a room by yourself, getting the angle right, getting the pressure right.

I went down onto porch boards I'd swept that morning, my cheek against a plank still faintly cold from the night, and the wicker chair went over with a sound like something small breaking, and above me his other hand was already moving, unhurried, the way a man moves through a task he's done a hundred times in rehearsal and only once for real. The chime went off hard on a wind that had come from nowhere and would be gone again in a minute. I heard the third step give, outside, under a weight that wasn't there — it had been giving for four years, and it finally, all the way, went.

The last true, exact thing I registered — because he had, after all, spent three months teaching me to notice exact things — was the smell he'd described that very first Wednesday in September. Dirt. Cut tomato vines, months dead. Somebody, somewhere, burning leaves.

Then the flat grey light did what it had been threatening to do since the first afternoon he sat in that chair and made me say I was her. It went out.

---

The house sold in March, faster than the agent expected, given everything. The woman who bought it — I won't use her name; that seems, given everything you've just read, like the wrong way to end this — found a stack of composition notebooks at the back of a kitchen drawer while she was lining the shelves with fresh paper, and being the kind of person who reads things left behind in houses, she read them, start to finish, over two nights, at the kitchen table, with all the lights on.

She told herself, the way you do, that it was fiction. Somebody's project. It read like fiction — too clean, too neatly shaped, real life doesn't usually organize its dread into sections with breaks between them the way this did.

She put the notebooks back in the drawer. She did not throw them away. She has thought, once or twice since, about why not, and has not arrived at an answer she likes.

On the second Wednesday in March, a man she'd never met knocked on the porch door and said a friend had told him about her — a friend from a group, he said, though he didn't say which one — and that he had something he needed to say out loud to somebody before he said it to the person who mattered, and would she mind, would she terribly mind, sitting somewhere and just listening while he tried to get it right.

She heard herself say, before she'd decided to say anything at all, that the wicker chair was the comfortable one, and that he should start wherever felt true, and that she'd want to know, above everything else — she surprised herself with how certain this came out, like a rule she'd always known instead of one she was making up on the spot — exactly what he could smell in the room, when it happened. Because that's where the truth keeps its splinters.

Outside, on the porch beam, the sea-glass chime that had come with the house, the one she'd meant a dozen times to ask the realtor about and never had, turned once on its little brass hooks, in a wind that hadn't been blowing a second before.
