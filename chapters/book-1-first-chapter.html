PART ONE: NORA

People think confession is supposed to be honest. In my experience, it's mostly rehearsed. I've run a business out of my sunporch for six years now called Second Draft. The pitch, if you can call it that — I've never once advertised, everyone who finds me is sent by somebody who found me first — is simple. Nobody says the true, hard thing right on the first try. Not the good ones and not the terrible ones. You think a man gets "I forgive you" right on the first attempt, forty years overdue, standing over his mother's casket? He needs a run at it first. A place to be clumsy where it doesn't cost him anything. That's what I sell: a room to be clumsy in. They sit across from me in the wicker chair that's outlasted two coats of porch paint and one marriage, and they tell somebody who isn't anybody the thing they can't yet tell the person who is. I ask the ordinary questions — where were you standing, what could you smell, what was she wearing, what time did the light come through the window — because detail is where the truth keeps its splinters. A lie stays smooth if you don't handle it too much. A true thing snags. I used to tell new clients, half joking — the way you joke about things you'd rather not think about too hard — that if you got the details exact enough, precise enough, true enough, you stopped rehearsing the moment and started building it. I don't say that anymore. Not since Roy. I don't ask for real names. That's rule one, and I tell every client on the phone before I ever see their face: use whatever name feels true, not whichever one's on your driver's license. It isn't only a legal thing. It's that people are braver behind a small mask than they are behind their own name, and I am in the business of borrowed courage. In six years, I have coached a man through telling his mother he's an atheist, a woman through firing the business partner who was also her college roommate, a teenage boy through coming out to a father who kept a shotgun mounted over the fireplace like a piece of framed art. I have played wives, husbands, dead fathers, living ones, a dog once, at a client's insistence, because he said the dog was the only one who'd actually understand him. Mostly, when they leave my porch, they aren't fixed. Rehearsal doesn't fix anything. It just means they don't flinch as hard when the curtain goes up for real. The town does its part too, I suppose, though I didn't think of it that way at the time. Six thousand people, give or take, spread along a river that floods every third or fourth spring, and a Main Street with more empty storefronts than full ones anymore. The kind of place where everybody's business is technically private and functionally public, where the woman at the bank knows your overdraft history and the man at the hardware store knows what you're building before you've bought the lumber for it. A town like that either drives its secrets underground or teaches people to carry them alone, and either way, eventually, somebody needs a room. Mine has a green mailbox at the end of the walk, hunter green, the good kind, and a dog three houses down that only ever barks at the mail truck, and eleven wooden steps up from the front walk, the third one from the bottom gone soft with frost heave for four years running, and a sea-glass chime on the porch beam that clinks against itself in any wind at all. I mention these things now because I didn't think to mention them, for a long time, to anyone. They were just the furniture of my life. It didn't occur to me that furniture could be evidence.
